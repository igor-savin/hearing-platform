    ["Why hearing deserves attention on your floor", "In most senior communities a meaningful share of residents own hearing aids that no longer work. Blocked with wax, out of battery, or never fitted properly in the first place. The aids end up in a drawer. The resident stops joining conversations, stops coming to activities, and starts being described as withdrawn or confused. It is rarely reported to staff, because residents do not experience it as a medical problem; they experience it as everyone else mumbling. The research points the same direction. [A 2012 study by Lin and Ferrucci in Archives of Internal Medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22371929/) associated every 10 decibels of hearing loss with roughly 1.4 times the odds of falling, with a mild loss carrying close to three times the odds. [The ACHIEVE randomized trial, published in The Lancet in 2023](https://www.achievestudy.org/key-findings), found 48% less cognitive decline over three years among participants already at higher risk who were treated for hearing loss. That is a result from one group within the trial rather than the trial as a whole, so a signal worth acting on rather than a settled conclusion."],
    ["Option one: we come to you", "An on-site visit runs 45 to 60 minutes in your activity room or common space. It usually opens with a short talk residents genuinely enjoy, on why hearing aids stop working and what to do about it, and then becomes a working clinic: cleaning, wax removal from the device, dome, tube, and filter changes, and function checks. We troubleshoot aids a resident has given up on. We work on any brand from any provider, including devices that were never purchased from us. We do not sell anything on site, which is the part most directors ask about first."],

Everyone is seen by a clinician rather than a technician, either an audiologist or our [Director of Hearing Aid Services](/about-us/lais-ama/). It doubles as an outing on your activities calendar, and it solves the transportation barrier that is the most common reason a resident quietly stops getting hearing care at all."],

Most communities start with a single on-site visit and add clinic days once they see how many residents turn out to have a fixable problem."],

Bring the hearing aids, even if they have not been worn in months, and even if you do not know where they came from. A device that has been written off as broken is very often just blocked with wax or holding no charge. Bring whatever paperwork exists, but do not delay the visit hunting for it. And come along to the appointment if you can. Families remember instructions the patient does not, and they are the ones who notice whether the aids are actually being worn afterwards. If your parent cannot travel easily, ask their community whether they would like us to visit; a great many are glad to have the programming, and the request often lands better coming from a resident's family than from us."],
