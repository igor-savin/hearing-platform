      ["One ear versus both", "Tinnitus that affects both ears fairly evenly is the usual presentation, and it often ties to age-related or noise-related hearing change spread across both sides. A sound locked to one ear is different. The auditory pathways on each side are largely separate, so a problem confined to one of them tends to point at something local: a specific ear canal, a specific eardrum, or the nerve serving that side. That asymmetry is why a clinician takes a one-sided complaint seriously rather than filing it alongside the everyday buzz many people learn to tune out. Getting clear on [what tinnitus is](/tinnitus/what-is-tinnitus/) helps explain why the side it lands on carries information."],
      ["Everyday causes of one-sided ringing", "A great deal of one-sided tinnitus has a plain, treatable source. Earwax pressing against the eardrum can set off ringing in that ear alone, which is why [earwax removal](/services/earwax-removal/) sometimes clears it entirely. A middle-ear infection, a lingering cold, or fluid behind one eardrum can do the same. So can a single loud exposure — a firecracker, a concert speaker, or a power tool held to one side — that stressed one ear more than the other. Jaw-joint tension and certain medications contribute for some people as well. Causes like these are common and manageable, which is part of why an evaluation tends to settle nerves rather than raise them."],
      ["When one-sided tinnitus is a red flag", "The reason clinicians don't wave off one-sided ringing is a small set of conditions that present this way. [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org) lists one-sided and pulsing tinnitus among the patterns that warrant a medical look. This does not mean the worst is likely, since most one-sided tinnitus is not serious. The point is that separating the benign from the meaningful takes an exam, not a guess."],
