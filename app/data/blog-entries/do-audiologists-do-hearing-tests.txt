The credential usually means a clinical doctorate, supervised clinical hours, and state licensure. In practice, that person is the one who runs your test, reads the [audiogram](/hearing-testing/), and turns a page of thresholds into a clear picture of how you are hearing.       ["The tests an audiologist runs", "A hearing test is really a small battery of measures, not a single beep check. Pure-tone audiometry maps the softest sounds you can detect across a range of pitches. Speech testing gauges how well you make out words at a comfortable volume, which often matters more in real life than the tones alone. Tympanometry gently varies pressure to see how your eardrum moves and whether the middle ear is clear. Depending on what turns up, the audiologist may add bone-conduction or other measures to locate where a problem sits."],

A hearing instrument specialist is licensed to test for the purpose of fitting hearing aids, but works within a narrower scope than a doctoral-level audiologist. An audiologist bridges the two: full diagnostic testing, non-medical management, and a referral to an ENT when the findings call for a physician."],
