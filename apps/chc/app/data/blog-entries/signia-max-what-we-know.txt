      ["The idea, in one paragraph", "Almost every hearing aid advertising artificial intelligence runs a single neural network trained to do one job: separate speech from noise. Signia's position with MaX is that one network is being asked to make too many decisions at once. Is this a conversation or background chatter? Is the voice it just found yours or somebody else's? Did the room change? A network optimised for noise reduction does not necessarily know, and when it guesses wrong it does the wrong helpful thing. It mutes a restaurant while you are trying to hear across the table, or ducks your own voice as you speak. So MaX splits the work across four purpose-trained networks and coordinates them. Signia calls the result Acoustic Intelligence. It is a genuine architectural position rather than marketing dressing, and it is different from the bet Oticon made two weeks earlier with Reveal, which runs two AI systems with deliberately opposing goals. We compare the two on our [MaX versus Reveal page](/products/hearing-aids-by-brand/signia/signia-max-vs-oticon-reveal/)."],

Both figures cite white papers by Jensen and colleagues, dated 2026. We went looking for those papers. They are Signia's own documents, not peer-reviewed publications, and they are not available to read anywhere we can find. The competitors are never named. The sample size, the test protocol and the outcome measure are not disclosed. Then there are the chip figures: 54 times more processing power, 50% greater energy efficiency, twice the memory. The footnote attached to that sentence reads, in full, \"Compared to Signia IX.\" That is not a citation. It is a baseline. And the claim that RealTime Conversation Enhancement now processes 75% more data points per second than IX carries no footnote at all. None of this makes Signia wrong. Internal validation and bench measurement are how hearing aids get engineered, and Signia's research group has a long and serious record. It does mean nobody outside the company has checked, and that the most impressive-sounding numbers describe silicon rather than what you will hear in a restaurant."],
